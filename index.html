Then and Now: The American Family

People often talk about how much families have changed, but how much, and in which direction? For each question, you’ll see where things stood about 50 years ago. Guess where they stand now, then check your guess against federal statistics and national surveys.

The fuller picture

Which “then” are we comparing to? Many comparisons, including the ones here, start in the 1960s or 1970s, close to an unusual moment. Americans married youngest in 1956, when the typical man married at 22.5 and the typical woman at 20.1. In 1890, the typical ages were 26.1 for men and 22.0 for women. Historian Stephanie Coontz’s book The Way We Never Were argues that the 1950s family many people picture as “traditional” was historically unusual. The starting year you pick shapes the story the trend seems to tell. You can see this in the “Age at marriage” chart above, which goes back to 1890.

Divorce: down, with one exception. The divorce rate fell from 22.6 per 1,000 married women around 1980 to 14.4 in 2023. Part of the reason is who marries: people with more education have become more likely than others to marry, and they are less likely to divorce. The exception is “gray divorce.” Among married women 50 and older, the rate rose from 3.9 per 1,000 in 1990 to 11.0 in 2008 and was 10.3 in 2023. What about “half of all marriages end in divorce”? Among Americans who married in the 1970s, 47% had seen their first marriage end in divorce by 2023. Among everyone who has ever married, it’s about one in three, though some of those married more recently will still divorce.

Births: later, fewer, and a plateau in unmarried births. The share of births to unmarried women peaked at 41.0% in 2009 and was 39.5% in 2024. “Unmarried” includes parents who live together without marrying. In 2024, the average woman having her first baby was 27.6, a record high. The total fertility rate was also a record low: if 2024’s birth rates held, women would have about 1.6 children each on average, below the roughly 2.1 needed for a population to replace itself without immigration. Births to teenagers hit a record low too, at 12.6 per 1,000 women ages 15 to 19.

Young adults at home. In 2025, 58% of 18- to 24-year-olds lived in their parents’ home, compared with 16% of 25- to 34-year-olds. One catch: the survey behind these numbers counts college students living in dorms as living with their parents, which inflates the figure for the younger group.

Dads: more involved, still not equal. In 2016, fathers spent about 8 hours a week on child care and 10 on housework, up from 2.5 and 4 in 1965. Mothers still did more: about 14 hours of child care and 18 of housework.

Attitudes changed along with behavior. In 1990, 63% of non-Black adults said they would oppose a close relative marrying a Black person. By 2016, 14% did.

Other fields to bring in. Economists study how women’s jobs and earnings changed what marriage offers both partners; among women ages 25 to 34, the share who were homemakers fell from 43% in 1975 to 14% in 2016. Demographers project how lower fertility will change the age structure of the population. Historians, like Coontz, ask which past we’re comparing ourselves to.

Talk about it

  1. Which guess was furthest off? Were your misses in a particular direction?
  2. Which changes here would you call problems, which would you call improvements, and which are neither? Would everyone in your class sort them the same way? Why or why not?
  3. Fewer households are married couples, but the divorce rate has fallen. How can both be true?
  4. In 1975, the most common path for 25- to 34-year-olds was moving out, working, marrying, and having kids. What has changed about the economy, education, and expectations that might explain why that path is less common now?
  5. Most comparisons here start around 1970. How might the story change if they started in 1900, or in 2000? Use the charts to check.
  6. In the “Children” chart, “two parents” includes stepparents, adoptive parents, and (since 2007) parents who live together without being married. How would the picture change if it counted only married biological parents? Which definition fits which question?

About the data

Each question uses the most recent figure available from a federal agency or a national research organization, so the “now” years differ: 2016 for fathers’ time and 2015 for intermarriage are the latest national figures I could find from those sources. The household, marriage-age, and living-at-home figures come from the Census Bureau’s Current Population Survey; birth figures come from birth certificates collected by the National Center for Health Statistics.

The charts use the Census Bureau’s historical tables on families and living arrangements (through 2025) and National Center for Health Statistics reports. Percentages are calculated from the counts in the Census tables. In years when the Census Bureau published both an original and a revised estimate, the revised one is shown. Dots mark years that stand alone, such as census years, and the lines connect across the gaps. The divorce figures in the “Divorce” chart leave out several states that don’t report divorces (in 2024: California, Hawaii, Indiana, Minnesota, and New Mexico).

How Have U.S. Families Changed?

People have strong opinions about what’s happened to American families, but fewer of us have seen the numbers. Make five guesses, then explore the trends yourself.

Guess 1 of 5

In 2025, the typical man marrying for the first time was almost 31. How old was the typical man at his first marriage in 1890?

The fuller picture

The numbers describe change. They don’t say whether it’s good or bad. Researchers disagree about what these trends mean for adults and children, and so does the public. In a 2023 Pew Research Center survey, 40% of Americans said they were pessimistic about the future of marriage and the family, and 25% were optimistic.

Talk about it

  1. Which answer surprised you most? Where did your guess come from: your own family, news, social media, TV?
  2. Many people think of the 1950s as the “traditional” family. What does the marriage-age chart suggest about how typical that decade was? What was going on in the 1950s that might explain it?
  3. The divorce rate has fallen, and so has the marriage rate. How could those two facts be connected? Who is getting married now, compared with a few decades ago?
  4. The Census Bureau counts stepparents, adoptive parents, and (since 2007) unmarried parents who live together as “two parents.” How would the picture change if it counted only married biological parents? Which definition is the right one, and for what purpose?
  5. More people live alone than ever. Is that a sign of isolation, independence, wealth, longer lives, or something else? What evidence would help you decide?
  6. Pick one trend. What changes in the economy, laws, technology, or beliefs might explain it? How could a researcher test your explanation?

About the data

Marriage age, children, households, and young adults: the U.S. Census Bureau’s historical tables on families and living arrangements, based on the Current Population Survey’s Annual Social and Economic Supplement (through 2025, released December 2025) and on earlier censuses. Percentages are calculated from the counts in those tables. In years when the Census Bureau published both an original and a revised estimate, the revised one is shown. Dots mark years that stand alone, such as census years; the lines connect across the gaps.

Divorce and marriage rates: the National Center for Health Statistics’ provisional counts per 1,000 people. The divorce figures leave out several states that don’t report divorces (in 2024: California, Hawaii, Indiana, Minnesota, and New Mexico), and the rate is calculated only for the states that do.

Births to unmarried women: the National Center for Health Statistics’ birth certificate data, 1940–2024.

Sources: U.S. Census Bureau, Table MS-2 (median age at first marriage), Table CH-1 (living arrangements of children), Tables HH-1, HH-4, and HH-6 (households by type and size), and Table AD-1 (young adults living at home). National Center for Health Statistics, Provisional Number of Marriages and Marriage Rate and Divorces and Rate: United States, 2000–2024; “Nonmarital Childbearing in the United States, 1940–99” (National Vital Statistics Reports 48(16), 2000); and the annual “Births: Final Data” reports for 2010 through 2024 (National Vital Statistics Reports 75(2), 2026). Pew Research Center, “Public Has Mixed Views on the Modern American Family” (2023).
